How Many Koi in a 500-Gallon Pond?

Published October 3, 2026. Built by Jeremy Panasuk.

Quick answer: SRAC Publication No. 7201, the koi and goldfish species profile, does not give a home-pond headcount or a gallons-per-koi figure. The number below is this site's aquarium stocking function pointed at 500 gallons on the heavy setting. It is not a pond stocking standard. Twenty-one fish in the calculator's XL class land at 98% of that capacity. Twenty-two land at 103% and overstocked. The limits that actually bind a pond are the water-quality numbers in SRAC 7201 and the Merck environmental chapter.

What SRAC 7201 does say about the fish

Koi are an ornamental variety of common carp, Cyprinus carpio. Goldfish are Carassius auratus. Both are temperate. Their optimum is about 65–75°F (18–24°C). They can survive temperatures as low as about 32°F (0°C) and as high as about 95°F (35°C), especially if the change is gradual and seasonal. Temperatures outside the optimum may suppress the immune system and decrease feeding and growth. Dissolved oxygen should be at least 5 mg/L, though koi and goldfish will tolerate much lower oxygen for short periods. Un-ionized ammonia and nitrite should be kept below 0.05 mg/L. pH should be as close to 7 as possible. They may tolerate a pH between 5 and 9.

Goldfish and common carp lack true stomachs, so in that production profile they benefit from multiple feedings a day. Most producers use a 25–32% protein diet. That feeding note is not the one-feeding-a-day maintenance schedule UF/IFAS VM114 gives for most pet animals. See how much to feed tropical fish for the split.

Common carp can grow to more than 4 feet (122 cm) and 80 pounds (36 kg). That is the species maximum in the profile, not a length this page assigns to a pet koi. This site's XL class starts at 10 inches. An adult that reaches the species maximum is past that class. The calculator has no larger class, so a fish headed past 10 inches is not "one XL point" for life. This page does not state a typical pet-koi length, because 7201 does not give one.

The aquarium function, applied to 500 gallons

Bioload points on this site are a planning estimate for a cycled freshwater aquarium, not a published pond standard. Heavy or messy setups use 0.6 points of capacity per gallon. XL fish are 14 points each. Enter 500 gallons and the heavy style on the stocking calculator if you want the same arithmetic.

Capacity is round(500 × 0.6) = 300 points. Twenty-one XL fish are 21 × 14 = 294 points, round(294 / 300 × 100) = 98%, status at capacity. Twenty-two XL fish are 308 points, round(308 / 300 × 100) = 103%, status overstocked.

At capacity means the function's own line: no new fish. It does not mean a 500-gallon pond is known to hold 21 koi. Overstocked at 22 is the same function stepping over 100%. If the fish will outgrow the 10-inch XL bin, 21 is already too many for this estimate, because each fish would need a class the calculator does not have.

The filter function on 500 gallons at the heavy level is 7 turnovers, an ideal 3,500 gallons per hour, and a derated rating of ceil(3500 × 1.2 / 5) × 5 = 4,200 gallons per hour. That is the aquarium filter function. It is not a pond-pump specification, and it says nothing about skimmers, waterfalls, or bottom drains.

The heater function is not run here. A pond that SRAC says can sit near 32°F through a gradual seasonal change is not an aquarium being lifted from room temperature to 78°F. Applying the wattage function to 500 gallons would pretend the pond needs an aquarium heater. It does not follow from 7201.

The numbers that do limit the pond

Keep un-ionized ammonia and nitrite under 0.05 mg/L, oxygen at least 5 mg/L, and pH near 7, from 7201. The Merck environmental chapter, written for aquatic systems generally, says dissolved oxygen above 5 mg/L is optimal for most finfish and that fish experience stress below 5 mg/L. A footnote on its reference table says pond oxygen fluctuates through the day, concentrations under 5 mg/L are dangerous, and mortality may be seen at 4 mg/L or below, depending on species and how long the drop lasts. Low dissolved oxygen and high ammonia are the two parameters that chapter says are most likely to kill fish directly. Causes of low oxygen in ponds, in that chapter, include excessive feeding and overstocking. Unrecognized overstocking can happen in a koi pond when fish spawn and the offspring stay in the system. A 98% calculator result does not budget for a spawn.

Nitrite toxicity can be corrected by a water change in a small tank. The same chapter says a water change may be impractical for a large pond. That is why the 0.05 mg/L nitrite ceiling in 7201 has to be watched before the pond is in trouble, not after a 60% aquarium water change would have been the fix. The 60% figure lives on the pet-fish checklist as an aquarium example. It is not a pond procedure.

A common mistake

Treating 21 XL fish at 98% as "how many koi fit in 500 gallons." The percent is the aquarium function. SRAC 7201 never published that headcount. A retailer gallons-per-fish rule is not used on this page, because that kind of figure is not in the sources above. The other mistake is stocking to the species maximum in your head — more than 4 feet — and still counting each animal as one 10-inch XL point.

Limits, and when to call a veterinarian

New koi are quarantined, in the Merck management chapter, for a minimum of 30 days at 24°C (75°F), because koi herpesvirus is reportable. Fish that become ill in that quarantine should be tested for it. Immune suppression outside 65–75°F is already in 7201, so a fish that stops eating in a cold snap is not automatically a stocking error, and it is not automatically fine. An aquatic veterinarian is the person who sorts a virus, a parasite, and a water-quality crash. This site does not cover reef or marine systems; koi are here only because the calculator can show its own ceiling and the extension sheet supplies the water limits.
