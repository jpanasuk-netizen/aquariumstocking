Why does bioload beat the inch-per-gallon rule?

The 1-inch-per-gallon rule dates from goldfish-bowl era advice. It ignores body mass, and it ignores that surface area limits oxygen exchange more than volume limits waste. Adult size is what counts. Bioload points on this calculator are small (≤2") = 1 pt, medium (2–5") = 3 pts, large (5–10") = 8 pts, XL (10"+) = 14 pts. A normal setup allows 1 point per gallon. A heavy-stock setup allows 0.6 points per gallon.

Common questions, answered

Is 1 inch of fish per gallon a good stocking rule?

No — it fails at both ends. A 10-inch oscar in a 10-gallon tank 'fits' the rule and dies in weeks; a dozen 1-inch neon tetras in 10 gallons is lightly stocked. Bioload scales with body mass and waste output, not length. Count adult size, use bioload points (small fish 1, medium 3, large 8, XL 14), and keep the total at or under capacity: 10 points per 10 gallons on a normal setup.

What wattage heater do I need for my aquarium?

For a rise above 9°F and at most 15°F, this calculator uses 4 watts per gallon and rounds up to the next standard size. A 20-gallon tank from 68°F to 78°F is 100 watts, one heater. A 55-gallon tank from 68°F to 78°F is 250 watts, and the two-heater note is 125 watts each.

How strong should my aquarium filter be?

On this calculator, 29 gallons at the normal level is 5 turnovers an hour, 145 gallons per hour ideal, and a rating of 175. The rating adds a 20% step and rounds up to the next 5 gallons per hour. That step is this site's allowance, not a manufacturer measurement.

How often should I change aquarium water?

On this calculator, a weekly nitrate rise of 20 ppm, a ceiling of 40 ppm, and tap water at 5 ppm returns a 60% water change. The plateau at that change is 38 ppm. Those three figures are example inputs, not a test from a tank.
